How the betting panels actually work
← Back to Sports

This site has two betting-adjacent panels: the MLB Value Screen, which compares an independently computed win-probability model against the live market to flag disagreements, and NFL Odds, which just shows live market odds with no model attached. This page explains exactly how each one works, where the numbers come from, and what the terminology means — including where the model is weak.

MLB · Model vs Market

MLB Value Screen

The idea: compute a win-probability estimate from MLB stats alone, with no knowledge of what the betting market thinks, then compare the two. When they disagree by enough, that's flagged as worth a second look — not a pick, just a place where the model and the market don't agree.

Data sources

  • Market odds — BetExplorer.com Scraped server-side by a Cloudflare Worker (/api/odds), because the browser can't fetch betexplorer.com directly (it doesn't send a CORS header). The scrape reads the site's MLB fixtures listing and converts its kickoff times from CEST to ET. Results are edge-cached for about 4 minutes. Known limitation: BetExplorer frequently hasn't posted lines for most of a day's slate yet, especially hours before first pitch — the screen can only price what BetExplorer has actually listed, and shows “no market line yet” for everything else. This is the single biggest reason the screen can look sparse.
  • Model inputs — statsapi.mlb.com (MLB's own public Stats API, CORS-open) Team season runs scored, runs allowed, and games played (for the win-expectancy math below), plus today's probable starting pitchers and their season pitching lines (runs allowed and innings pitched, to compute RA9 — runs allowed per 9 innings).

Terminology

Fair %
The market's implied win probability with the sportsbook's built-in margin (the “vig”) removed. Raw moneyline odds always imply more than 100% combined across both teams — that extra is the house's cut. Fair% rescales the pair so they sum to exactly 100%, giving the market's actual best guess with the profit margin stripped out.
Model %
This site's own from-scratch win-probability estimate (see the formula below). It's computed without looking at the market at all.
Edge
Model% − Fair%. Positive means the model likes that team more than the market does; negative means less.
Call
Value edge ≥ +4 points — model favors this side noticeably more than the market.
Fade edge ≤ −4 points — model favors this side noticeably less than the market.
Check |edge| ≥ 10 points — the disagreement is large enough that it's more likely the model is missing something (a stale stat, an unannounced lineup change) than that it's found a genuinely huge edge. Treat as “look into this,” not “bet this.”
No tag (shown as NO EDGE) — model and market are within 4 points of each other. No disagreement worth flagging.

How Model % is actually computed

There are two versions, and the screen automatically falls back from the first to the second:

v2 — starter-adjusted (used whenever both probable starters have thrown at least 20 innings this season): each team's expected runs in this specific matchup is that team's season runs-scored-per-game, scaled by how the opposing starter's runs-allowed rate compares to the league average:

awayExpectedRuns = (away team's RS per game) × (home starter's RA9 ÷ league RS per game) homeExpectedRuns = (home team's RS per game) × (away starter's RA9 ÷ league RS per game) P(away wins) = 1 / (1 + (homeExpectedRuns / awayExpectedRuns) ^ 1.83)

That win probability is converted to a percentage, then a flat 4-point home-field adjustment is applied — added to the home team's number, subtracted from the away team's — before the pair is shown as Model%. The 1.83 exponent is the standard baseball Pythagorean-expectation constant (a fixed historical fit between run differential and win rate, not something recalculated per season). “League RS per game” itself isn't a hardcoded number — it's computed fresh each time from that day's full MLB standings (total runs scored across the league ÷ total games played).

v1 — season Pythagorean fallback (used when a probable starter is unannounced, or hasn't thrown 20 innings yet): skips the starter-matchup adjustment entirely and just uses each team's own season-long Pythagorean win expectancy, independently:

P(team wins) = 1 / (1 + (runs allowed / runs scored) ^ 1.83)

Same home +4 / away −4 adjustment is applied on top. This version knows nothing about who's actually pitching — it's a coarser, whole-season-average estimate used only when the starter data isn't reliable yet.

What the model doesn't see: bullpen quality, ballpark factors, weather, lineup changes, or injuries beyond what's baked into season stats. It also doesn't know anything about the market — Model% and Fair% are computed completely independently and only compared at the very end. A large Edge is exactly as likely to mean “the model found something real” as “the model is working from stale or incomplete data” — that's what the CHECK tag exists to flag.
NFL · Market Lines

NFL Odds

There's no NFL version of the MLB model above — there's no equivalent of a “starter” to adjust for, and BetExplorer (the MLB market source) has no NFL/American football section at all. So this panel is deliberately simpler: it shows live market odds only, with no independent model and no edge/call system.

Data source

  • ESPN's own summary endpoint (site.api.espn.com, the same host every other page on this site already uses, CORS-open — no worker proxy needed). Each game's pickcenter field carries a DraftKings-sourced moneyline. Unlike BetExplorer, ESPN's coverage is essentially complete for the whole week's NFL slate, posted well ahead of kickoff.

Terminology

ML
Moneyline, in standard American odds format: a + number is the profit on a $100 bet if that team wins (the underdog); a − number is how much you'd need to stake to profit $100 (the favorite).
Win %
The same de-vig math as MLB's Fair% above, applied to the moneyline pair — the market's implied win probability with the sportsbook's margin removed. There's no Model% to compare it against here; it's shown purely as a translation of the odds into a probability.
